## Q1. Empirical evidence on corporate hedging value (airlines, food/agri, selective hedging/speculation)

### Takeaway
The best-identified evidence says hedging creates value mainly by lowering financial-distress and underinvestment costs, and most of that value goes to highly levered or financially constrained firms (Gilje & Taillard 2017; Carter-Rogers-Simkins 2006; Cornaggia 2013). Airline studies after 2015 disagree on whether a "hedging premium" exists. Selective hedging, meaning using views to adjust hedge ratios, is common, but firms show no skill at it. It also shows behavioral signatures such as overconfidence and self-attribution. Large hedging losses (Southwest 2015-16, the 2020 COVID over-hedging) show that volume risk and mark-to-market risk can make a "hedge" add risk.

### Cited Findings

**A. Causal / well-identified evidence on hedging and firm value**
- [PR] Gilje, E.P. & Taillard, J.P. (2017). "Does Hedging Affect Firm Value? Evidence from a Natural Experiment." *Review of Financial Studies* 30(12): 4083-4132. DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhx069 (verified). The authors use a basis-risk shock as exogenous variation: Canadian light-oil producers lost part of their ability to hedge relative to US producers. Treated firms lost value, mainly those with high ex-ante leverage. The paper names lower distress probability and less underinvestment risk as the main channels — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v30y2017i12p4083-4132..html); [WP version, Wharton](https://rodneywhitecenter.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/23.14.Gilje_.pdf)
- [PR-found.] Cornaggia, J. (2013). "Does risk management matter? Evidence from the U.S. agricultural industry." *Journal of Financial Economics* 109(2): 419-440 (verified). Triple-difference tests around shifts in the supply of risk-management instruments (crop insurance) show risk management goes with higher productivity. The link is stronger for group-performance-linked instruments and for producers in counties with high bank deposits, a proxy for access to finance — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v109y2013i2p419-440.html). Counterpoint: Vigani & Kathage (2019, *American Journal of Agricultural Economics* 101(5): 1432-1454) find farm-level productivity effects of risk management are mixed, positive or negative depending on strategy — [glos eprint](https://eprints.glos.ac.uk/6861/5/6861%20Vigani%20%282019%29%20To%20risk%20or%20not%20to%20risk.pdf)
- [PR] Meta-analyses: Geyer-Klingeberg, Hang & Rathgeber (2020, *European Journal of Finance*) on the hedging premium, cited as reviewing studies with firm FE/endogeneity controls; Hang et al. (2020, *International Journal of Finance & Economics*), a meta-analysis of 411 studies finding that capital structure mediates the hedging-value link. Both are known only via secondary search snippets in this session, so exact titles and volumes are [UNVERIFIED] — [search reference via SUFE/IDEAS listing](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/353390)
- [PR-found.][UNVERIFIED] Jin, Y. & Jorion, P. (2006). "Firm Value and Hedging: Evidence from U.S. Oil and Gas Producers." *Journal of Finance* 61(2). Widely cited as finding no value premium for hedging among oil and gas *producers*, where investors may want the commodity exposure. Not re-confirmed this session.
- [PR-found.][UNVERIFIED] Pérez-González, F. & Yun, H. (2013). "Risk Management and Firm Value: Evidence from Weather Derivatives." *Journal of Finance* 68(5). Widely cited as finding that weather-derivative availability raised the value of weather-exposed utilities. Not re-confirmed this session.
- [PR-found.][UNVERIFIED] Froot, Scharfstein & Stein (1993, *Journal of Finance*, "Risk Management: Coordinating Corporate Investment and Financing Policies") is the canonical theory: hedging is valuable when external finance is costly and it protects internal funds for investment. This is the mechanism Gilje & Taillard and Cornaggia support empirically.

**B. Airline fuel hedging (analog for procurement hedging of an input)**
- [PR-found.] Carter, D.A., Rogers, D.A. & Simkins, B.J. (2006). "Does Hedging Affect Firm Value? Evidence from the US Airline Industry." *Financial Management* 35(1): 53-86 (verified). Original "jet-fuel hedging premium" paper — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/fma/fmanag/carteretal06.html). [UNVERIFIED magnitude] It is commonly cited as a ~5-10% Tobin's Q premium, attributed to protecting investment capacity (buying distressed assets when fuel prices spike). A practitioner companion piece is in *Journal of Applied Corporate Finance* 18(4): 21-33 (2006) — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jacrfn/v18y2006i4p21-33.html)
- [PR] Treanor, S.D., Rogers, D.A., Carter, D.A. & Simkins, B.J. (2014). "Exposure, hedging, and value: New evidence from the U.S. airline industry." *International Review of Financial Analysis* 34: 200-211 (verified). Revisits the CRS (2006) premium and links value to the interaction between exposure and hedging policy — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finana:v:34:y:2014:i:c:p:200-211). [UNVERIFIED detail] It is commonly summarized as finding that hedging adds value mainly when fuel-price exposure is high, and that the premium is smaller than in CRS (2006).
- [PR] Treanor, Simkins, Rogers & Carter (2014). "Does Operational and Financial Hedging Reduce Exposure? Evidence from the U.S. Airline Industry." *Financial Review* 49(1): 149-172 (verified). Both financial hedging and operational hedging (fleet diversity and fuel efficiency) reduce jet-fuel exposure — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/finrev/v49y2014i1p149-172.html)
- [PR] A 2022 *Journal of Air Transport Management* study on the global airline industry ("The economic value rationale of fuel hedging: An empirical perspective from the global airline industry") concludes hedging works as risk mitigation but does not raise airline firm value. Authors, volume and DOI could not be retrieved (publisher returned 403), so treat it as partially verified — [ScienceDirect abstract page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0969699722001430)
- [Thesis, not PR] A 2020 USF dissertation ("Does Hedging Success Matter? An Empirical Study of Jet Fuel Hedging in the U.S. Airline Industry") finds realized hedge gains/losses relate *negatively* to firm value, turning positive only after outliers are removed. This shows how fragile the airline premium is — [USF Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/etd/8225/)
- [PR] Agricultural-economics journal article (2016, vol. 55 no. 1, "Airline Fuel Hedging", via AgEcon Search). Exact title and authors were not retrieved — [AgEcon Search PDF](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/262651/files/2016v55n1_03_AirlineFuelHedging.pdf)
- [PRESS] Southwest Airlines: saved about $3.5bn on fuel from 1999 to mid-2008 through long-dated hedges, then lost more than $1bn over about 18 months in 2015-16 ($577m incremental fuel expense in 2015, $0.31/gal; $484m in H1 2016, $0.49/gal) — [Motley Fool, 2016](https://www.fool.com/investing/2016/07/25/southwest-airlines-gets-burned-by-fuel-hedges-agai.aspx). The Southwest treasurer reportedly estimated net savings of about $2bn over 2001-2015 — same source/commentary. Net totals depend on window and method, and should be verified against 10-Ks.
- [PRESS] COVID-19 (2020): European airlines (IAG, Air France, Ryanair, easyJet) booked about $4.6bn of hedging losses and Asia-Pacific carriers (SIA, Emirates, Qantas, Cathay) about $3.2bn. IAG recorded a €1,781m MTM loss in 2020, Lufthansa €764m of over-hedging losses in Q3, and SIA a $710m Q1 MTM loss with nearly all of its fleet grounded. The cause was a demand/volume collapse that left carriers over-hedged. Responses included cutting hedge ratios (one carrier from ~90% to 40%) and moving to options and collars. US carriers that hedged lightly after the 2014 crash were largely spared — [EuroFinance](https://www.eurofinance.com/news/european-airlines-may-quit-fuel-hedging-after-4-66-billion-in-losses); [FlightGlobal on IAG](https://flightglobal.com/strategy/losses-prompt-iag-review-of-fuel-hedging/140901.article); [Reuters via Investing.com](https://m.uk.investing.com/news/commodities-news/steep-capacity-cut-leaves-airlines-with-overhedged-jet-fuel-headache-2078292); [Aviation News on SIA](https://aviationnews-online.com/public/article/singapore-airlines-records-710-million-q1-hedging-loss)

**C. Food/beverage firms (company self-description, not value evidence)**
- [CO] Starbucks: it manages market risk under an "umbrella risk management policy" in which risks are quantified and evaluated for mitigation. The FY2008 10-K says the policy restricts tolerated market risk before approved hedges are used and *prohibits speculative trading*. Green coffee is bought mostly via fixed-price and price-to-be-fixed purchase commitments (normal-purchase exemption, not marked to market), supplemented by financial commodity hedges. Maximum hedge tenor was 5 years (FY2011), tightened to 3 years for most instruments (FY2020). FY2022 sensitivity: a 10% move gives $3.0m earnings impact and $74m OCI impact for commodity hedges — [FY2008 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000829224/000095013408021134/v50316e10vk.htm); [FY2011 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000829224/000119312511317175/d232803d10k.htm); [FY2020 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/829224/000082922420000078/sbux-20200927.htm); [FY2022 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/829224/000082922422000058/sbux-20221002.htm)
- [CO] Mondelez (ex-Kraft snacks): Item 7A gives a VaR table with "Pre-Tax Earnings Impact" and "Fair Value Impact" columns for commodity instruments. VaR is described as maximum expected one-day loss at a stated confidence level under normal markets, not an expected loss — [FY2017 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001103982/000119312518037332/d466496d10k.htm). The FY2025 10-K (filed Feb 2026) names cocoa costs and the ability to hedge them as a key risk factor — [BoardroomAlpha mirror](https://www.boardroomalpha.com/sec/mdlz-10-k-2026-02-04-0001628280-26-005345). The confidence level and cocoa-specific VaR figures were not extracted.

**D. Selective hedging and speculation by non-financial firms**
- [PR-found.] Brown, G.W., Crabb, P.R. & Haushalter, D. (2006). "Are Firms Successful at Selective Hedging?" *Journal of Business* 79(6): 2925-2950 (verified). In 44 gold miners, firms cut hedges when prices moved against them. Together with survey evidence, this shows firms try to time markets, but "economic gains to selective hedging are small" and there is no evidence of better operating or financial performance — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jnlbus/v79y2006i6p2925-2950.html); [Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/are-firms-successful-at-selective-hedging/)
- [PR-found.][UNVERIFIED detail] Adam, T.R. & Fernando, C.S. (2006). "Hedging, speculation, and shareholder value." *Journal of Financial Economics* 81(2). It is cited in this session's sources as finding "considerable evidence of selective hedging" in gold producers — [Adam-Fernando-Salas WP, HU Berlin](https://www.wiwi.hu-berlin.de/en/Professorships/bwl/finance/Archiv/workingpapers/why-do-firms-engage-in-selective-hedging.pdf). From background knowledge, it found that derivative cash-flow gains came from a persistent forward risk premium (systematic contango/backwardation), not from timing skill. Verify.
- [PR] Adam, T.R., Fernando, C.S. & Salas, J.M. (2017). "Why do firms engage in selective hedging? Evidence from the gold mining industry." *Journal of Banking & Finance* (cited in the WP listing). In North American gold miners there is no evidence that selective hedging is more common among financially constrained firms (contra the "gamble for resurrection" view), and selective hedging is negatively related to firm size — [HU Berlin WP listing](https://www.wiwi.hu-berlin.de/en/Professorships/bwl/finance/Archiv/workingpapers/why-do-firms-hedge-selectively-evidence-from-the-gold-mining-industry); [Wharton WIFPR WP](https://wifpr.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/15-07.pdf). Exact JBF volume [UNVERIFIED].
- [PR] Adam, T.R., Fernando, C.S. & Golubeva, E. (2015). "Managerial overconfidence and corporate risk management." *Journal of Banking & Finance* 60: 195-208 (verified). Managers respond *asymmetrically* to speculative gains and losses from market timing: after gains they speculate more, consistent with self-attribution bias. The authors conclude overconfidence shapes risk management decisions — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbfina:v:60:y:2015:i:c:p:195-208)
- [PR] Bartram, S.M. (2019). "Corporate hedging and speculation with derivatives." *Journal of Corporate Finance* 57: 9-34 (verified). Across 6,896 firms in 47 countries the evidence "strongly suggests hedging motives" and finds no evidence of speculation with derivatives by country or derivative type — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/eee/corfin/v57y2019icp9-34.html); [Warwick WRAP PDF](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/92709/7/WRAP-corporate-hedging-speculation-derivatives-Bartram-2017.pdf)
- [PR] Out-of-sample tests on 24 commodities "do not favor selective hedging over traditional hedging as the former increases risk without delivering additional returns". The paper was surfaced in search but its authors and venue were not confirmed [UNVERIFIED citation].
- [PR] Brazilian firms in the 2008 crisis ("Hedging, selective hedging, or speculation?", *Journal of Multinational Financial Management*, 2013) studies large FX-derivative losses at Brazilian corporates. Findings were not extracted — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1042444X13000364)
- [PR] MDPI *Journal of Risk and Financial Management* 12(2):71 (2019), "Does Managerial Power Increase Selective Hedging? Evidence from the Oil and Gas Industry" — [MDPI](https://mdpi.com/1911-8074/12/2/71). Lower-tier journal; findings not extracted.
- [PR-found.][UNVERIFIED detail] Géczy, Minton & Schrand (2007). "Taking a View: Corporate Speculation, Governance, and Compensation." *Journal of Finance* 62(5). Its existence is confirmed via a reference list in this session. It is commonly summarized as finding that a meaningful share of derivative users at least occasionally take views, and that speculators tend to have stronger monitoring and incentive structures. Verify before citing numbers.
- [PR-found.][UNVERIFIED] Stulz (1996), "Rethinking Risk Management," *Journal of Applied Corporate Finance*. The classic argument is that selective hedging is justified only when a firm has a genuine information advantage, which a typical procurement team does not have in deep, liquid markets.

### Inferences
- For a procurement desk (an edible-oil refiner or food manufacturer), the academic case for hedging is the *distress/underinvestment channel*. The case is strongest for levered, margin-thin firms that cannot pass input costs through to customers. It is weak for firms with pass-through pricing or strong balance sheets.
- "Hedge to a view" (selective hedging) has no evidence of positive alpha. The gold-mining literature, the best data available for this, shows timing attempts, small gains and overconfidence dynamics. A heuristic system that changes hedge ratios on valuation signals should therefore be treated as *speculation needing its own risk budget and performance attribution*, not as hedging.
- Airline episodes show the main practical failure mode is **volume uncertainty, not price direction**. Hedge ratios set on forecast volumes became over-hedges when demand collapsed in 2020. Options and collars, plus hedge ratios scaled to firm-volume confidence, are the documented industry response.
- Mark-to-market optics and peer behavior strongly affect program survival (Southwest de-emphasized hedging after 2015-16; IAG cut its hedge ratio). This is consistent with regret and accountability effects, though no study isolates that cause.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed firm-level study of edible-oil refiners' or palm-oil buyers' hedging value (e.g., Unilever, Nestlé palm oil) was found. Unilever and Nestlé annual-report disclosures were not retrieved.
- No 2023-2026 peer-reviewed airline hedging-value study was confirmed. Several exist as theses or working papers.
- Exact magnitudes in CRS (2006) and Treanor et al. (2014) were not verified from full text.

## Q2. How major agribusinesses describe their risk management (10-Ks / annual reports)

### Takeaway
ADM and Bunge describe near-identical frameworks: a daily-measured *net* commodity position, controlled by **volumetric limits plus VaR limits**, with stress tests and a disclosed 10% adverse-move sensitivity. These are merchant/processor books managed against a market-risk budget, not pure procurement hedges. Cargill (private) and Wilmar (SGX-listed) disclosures could not be extracted this session.

### Cited Findings
- [CO] ADM FY2024 10-K (Item 7A): ADM manages commodity risk on its net commodity position and monitors exposure with metrics consisting of **volumetric limits and value-at-risk (VaR) limits**. It reports sensitivity as the loss in fair value from a hypothetical 10% adverse price change. The highest weekly net position had fair value $543m and market risk $54m at 31 Dec 2024, versus $498m and $50m at 31 Dec 2023 — [SEC EDGAR, ADM 10-K FY2024](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/7084/000000708425000011/adm-20241231.htm). Average/low positions and any explicit VaR dollar figure were not extracted.
- [CO] Bunge 10-Ks (FY2013-FY2022, consistent language): limits on unhedged positions combine **volumetric and VaR limits**. The net agricultural commodity position is measured daily, and "VaR is calculated on the net position and monitored at the 95% confidence interval." Scenario analysis and stress tests, including a hypothetical 10% adverse move, are used alongside VaR. The tables report high/low daily position values with market risk: FY2019 high $603m fair value / $(60)m risk, FY2018 high $2,131m / $(213)m — [Bunge FY2018 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1144519/000114451919000006/bg-12312018x10k.htm); [FY2019 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001144519/000114451920000004/bg-12312019x10k.htm); [FY2022 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1144519/000114451923000010/bg-20221231.htm). The VaR holding period (e.g., one-day) was not confirmed.
- [CO] Wilmar International: annual reports for FY2024 and FY2025 contain a "Risk Management" section (p.62, Governance), but its commodity-limit content was not extracted. The FY2015 report says Wilmar fully hedged operating FX exposure in IDR, MYR and CNY against USD from early 2015 — [Wilmar AR2015 (SGX)](https://links.sgx.com/1.0.0/corporate-announcements/00H49BE25TIKUZI2/396787_Wilmar%20International%20Limited%20Annual%20Report%202015.pdf); [AR2024](https://links.sgx.com/1.0.0/corporate-announcements/AMB8JY500N5OG4SQ/837839_Wilmar%20Annual%20Report%202024.pdf); [AR2025](https://links.sgx.com/1.0.0/corporate-announcements/CM05QAJQQWSH1PK6/880339_Wilmar%20Annual%20Report%202025.pdf)
- [CO] Starbucks and Mondelez as consumer-goods procurement analogs (see Q1-C): an umbrella policy, a speculation prohibition, tenor caps (3-5 yrs), use of normal-purchase forward contracts plus financial hedges, and VaR or 10%-sensitivity disclosure.

### Inferences
- The industry "control template" is (1) a net-position definition (physical plus derivatives), (2) a daily mark, (3) volumetric limits, (4) a VaR limit at about 95%, (5) stress and 10% sensitivity tests, and (6) a written prohibition on speculation. That last item sits uneasily with the selective-hedging behavior that academic studies document.
- The disclosed 10%-move market risk of about $50-60m against multi-billion revenue suggests agribusiness merchants run small *net* open positions relative to throughput. Their margin comes from basis, logistics and crush spreads, not outright price direction. A procurement model for a refiner should likewise separate flat-price risk from basis/spread risk.

### Gaps
- Cargill is private and files no 10-K. Its annual-report risk language was not retrieved.
- Wilmar's risk-management text, ADM's VaR figures and Bunge's VaR horizon were not extracted from the filings.
- No independent evidence was found on whether these limit frameworks improve outcomes.

## Q3. Mean reversion to cost/fundamentals vs. speculative/expectations-driven deviations ("classical vs Keynesian" lenses)

### Takeaway
Long-run data support *slow* reversion toward trend and costs, with long, large super-cycle departures. Over procurement horizons of months to a few years, though, prices are close to a random walk. The structural evidence mostly attributes the big 2003-08 moves to fundamentals (demand, storage) rather than speculation. Financialization changed co-movement and risk-sharing, but the evidence that index money distorted price *levels* is weak and contested. In "classical" terms, a cost or fundamental anchor exists but pulls weakly and with long, uncertain lags. The "Keynesian/expectations" view finds support mainly in short episodes and in co-movement, not in persistent level mispricing.

### Cited Findings

**Long-run reversion / cycles**
- [PR] Jacks, D.S. (2019). "From boom to bust: a typology of real commodity prices in the long run." *Cliometrica* 13(2): 201-220. DOI 10.1007/s11698-018-0173-5 (verified). For 40 commodities over 1900-2015, real prices rose modestly after 1950 and show recurring medium-run cycles with large, long-lived departures from trend, plus embedded boom/bust episodes. The WP version links longer and larger booms/busts to floating exchange-rate periods — [NBER w18874](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18874). A Princeton dataset update (42 commodities, to 2023) dates three complete super-cycles, the last one 1996 → peak 2010 → "near its trough" — [Princeton DSS](https://dss.princeton.edu/catalog/resource7549)
- [Secondary/PR] A 52-commodity dataset from 1265 to 2017 reports "a strong and robust long-run reversal effect" — [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/2019/03/08/seven-centuries-of-commodity-reversals). The underlying paper's authors and venue were not confirmed in this session [UNVERIFIED citation].
- [Secondary] Reversion is reportedly stronger in spot than in futures markets (Chaves & Viswanathan, 2016), as cited in a practitioner review — [FPA Journal 2022](https://www.financialplanningassociation.org/learning/publications/journal/OCT22-how-do-commodities-fit-client-portfolios-OPEN). The original citation is [UNVERIFIED].
- [Book, academic] Pirrong, C. (2011). *Commodity Price Dynamics: A Structural Approach*, Cambridge UP. Structural storage models show prices "nearly like a random walk, with very slight evidence of mean reversion". The copper analysis rejects speculation as the driver of 2005-09 copper prices — [author PDF](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/spirrong/pirrong_commodity_book_110310.pdf)
- [WP/NBER chapter] "Bubble Troubles? Rational Storage, Mean Reversion and Runs in Commodity Prices" (NBER w19037; NBER chapter c12812) argues storage's dampening effect, not rational or irrational speculation, drives observed reversion — [NBER w19037](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19037/w19037.pdf)
- [PR-found.][UNVERIFIED] Theoretical anchors: Working (1949) and the theory of storage, Deaton & Laroque (1992, *Review of Economic Studies*), the competitive storage model with occasional spikes when stocks run out, and Keynes (1930), normal backwardation, where hedgers pay speculators a risk premium. These frame the "classical" (cost/storage equilibrium) and "Keynesian" (risk premium/expectations) lenses.

**Speculation / financialization**
- [PR] Kilian, L. & Murphy, D.P. (2014). "The Role of Inventories and Speculative Trading in the Global Market for Crude Oil." *Journal of Applied Econometrics* 29(3): 454-478. DOI 10.1002/jae.2322 (verified). Using inventories to identify speculative (expectations-driven) demand shocks, the estimates rule out speculation and unexpected supply shortfalls as causes of the 2003-08 surge, which reflected unexpected demand growth from the global business cycle. Speculative demand shifts *did* matter in 1979, 1986 and 1990 — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:japmet:v:29:y:2014:i:3:p:454-478)
- [PR] Cheng, I.-H. & Xiong, W. (2014). "The Financialization of Commodity Markets." *Annual Review of Financial Economics* 6 (venue from background; PDF verified). Financialization substantially changed commodity markets through risk-sharing and information-discovery channels, and the review does not settle the bubble question — [Princeton PDF](https://www.princeton.edu/~wxiong/papers/Review_Financialization.pdf); [NBER appendix](https://data.nber.org/data-appendix/w19642/commodity_review6g.pdf)
- [PR] Irwin, S.H. & Sanders, D.R. (2011). "Index Funds, Financialization, and Commodity Futures Markets." *Applied Economic Perspectives and Policy* 33(1): 1-31 (EconPapers ID verified). The literature split between studies finding index effects on price levels and those finding no systematic relation casts "considerable doubt" on the index-bubble claim — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:apecpp:v:33:y:2011:i:1:p:1-31). Their OECD study finds no bubble caused by index/swap funds in 2006-08 and no significant link to volatility — [OECD](https://www.oecd.org/agriculture-and-food/the-impact-of-index-and-swap-funds-on-commodity-futures-markets_5kmd40wl1t5f-en)
- [PR] Main, S., Irwin, S.H., Sanders, D.R. & Smith, A. (2018). *Journal of Commodity Markets* 10: 22-28 (EconPapers ID verified). The average unconditional return to individual commodity futures is approximately zero both before and after financialization — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jocoma:v:10:y:2018:i:c:p:22-28). Implication: no systematic risk premium for long hedgers or investors to harvest or pay.
- [WP] Yan, Irwin & Sanders (2016, NCCC-134 conference) use index-roll and rebalancing events for identification and summarize prior work as giving "limited evidence of financialization impacts" — [farmdoc PDF](https://farmdoc.illinois.edu/assets/meetings/nccc134/conf_2016/pdf/Yan_Irwin_Sanders_NCCC-134_2016.pdf)
- [PR/WP] Fishe & Smith, "Do Speculators Drive Commodity Prices Away From Fundamentals?", find no evidence of the excess reversals that speculator-driven mispricing would imply — [UC Davis PDF](https://arefiles.ucdavis.edu/uploads/filer_public/e7/80/e7805170-bc16-4683-b3c4-52b6b0b34a96/speculation_fishe_smith_final.pdf). Final journal venue [UNVERIFIED; possibly *Journal of Commodity Markets*].
- [Contra view] Tang & Xiong (index investment raised co-movement of non-energy commodities with oil) and Singleton (investor flows and the 2008 oil boom/bust, *Management Science* 2014 [venue UNVERIFIED]) argue financial flows mattered. Büyükşahin & Robe (2011) find index activity is not associated with rising correlations — cited in [OECD/Irwin-Sanders](https://oecd-ilibrary.org/the-impact-of-index-and-swap-funds-on-commodity-futures-markets_5kmd40wl1t5f.pdf); [Singleton paper via EIA](https://www.eia.gov/finance/markets/reports_presentations/Paper-Boom%20and%20Bust%20in%20Oil%20Prices-Singleton.pdf)

### Inferences
- A "classical" cost-of-production or fundamental-value anchor is defensible over multi-year or decade horizons (Jacks's cycles, long-run reversal). Its pull is too slow and noisy to time purchases over a 3-18-month procurement window, where near-random-walk behavior dominates (Pirrong).
- The "Keynesian/expectations" lens (speculative demand, sentiment, flows) is best supported for specific episodes (oil 1979/1986/1990 per Kilian-Murphy) and for cross-commodity co-movement after 2004. It is weakly supported as a persistent level distortion that a buyer could exploit.
- Since average futures risk premia are about zero (Main et al. 2018), a systematic always-hedge policy has roughly zero expected cost in price terms. The decision should then rest on variance and distress reduction (Q1) and on transaction and margin-liquidity costs, not on predicted direction.

### Gaps
- No 2020-2026 peer-reviewed study was found specifically on mean reversion of *vegetable-oil* (palm, soybean oil) prices to production cost.
- Exact authorship of the seven-century reversal study and of Chaves & Viswanathan (2016) is unverified.
- Post-2020 financialization evidence (2021-22 energy and food spike) was not covered in the sources retrieved.

## Q4. Value-investing principles (Buffett/Munger/Lynch-style heuristics) in commodity procurement or commodity investing

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed study applying Buffett/Munger/Lynch heuristics such as "margin of safety" to commodity procurement was found. The closest rigorous analog is the commodity "value" factor (multi-year price reversal) in Asness-Moskowitz-Pedersen (2013). It earns a positive but modest and noisy premium that weakened after publication. Buffett's own commodity episode (silver, 1997-98) was a supply-deficit thesis, and he described it as a small gain.

### Cited Findings
- [PR-found.] Asness, C.S., Moskowitz, T.J. & Pedersen, L.H. (2013). "Value and Momentum Everywhere." *Journal of Finance* 68(3) (June 2013) (verified issue/date). Value and momentum premia show up across eight asset classes including commodities, with value and momentum negatively correlated. Value in commodities was new to the literature — [NYU PDF](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~lpederse/papers/ValMomEverywhere.pdf). [UNVERIFIED construction detail] From background knowledge, commodity "value" is the log of the average spot price 4.5-5.5 years ago over today's price, i.e., a 5-year reversal "cheapness vs. history" signal rather than a cost-based intrinsic value. Check the paper.
- [Practitioner/secondary] Post-publication (July 2013 onward), the combined "everywhere" value-momentum long/short averaged 0.48% with 2.36% volatility (Sharpe about 0.20), well below in-sample — [Alpha Architect / AlphaMaven re-test](https://alpha-maven.com/news/managed-futures/are-value-and-momentum-still-everywhere); see also [Larry Swedroe, Evidence Investor](https://www.evidenceinvestor.com/?p=21155). This is not commodity-specific and is a practitioner analysis.
- [PRESS/CO] Berkshire Hathaway silver (1997-98): a Feb 1998 release disclosed 129,710,000 oz bought between 25 Jul 1997 and 12 Jan 1998, less than 2% of the investment portfolio and about 25% of annual mine supply. The thesis was that demand exceeded production plus recycling, so inventories were being drawn down. At the 2006 AGM Buffett said "We did make a few dollars on it" — [Northern Miner](https://secure.northernminer.com/news/warren-buffet-takes-stake-in-silver/1000100046); [SD Bullion summary](https://sdbullion.com/blog/warren-buffett-silver-hoard-1997-to-2006). The original Berkshire letter wording was not retrieved [UNVERIFIED quote of the letter].
- [Practitioner/secondary] Long-run reversal across 52 commodities over seven centuries (see Q3) is the empirical backbone for a "buy below long-run real average" rule — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/2019/03/08/seven-centuries-of-commodity-reversals)
- [PR] Main et al. (2018): about zero average futures returns pre- and post-financialization (see Q3). A buyer cannot rely on a structural risk premium. Any edge from "cheapness" rules has to come from reversal or value timing, which is noisy.

### Inferences
- A defensible translation of "margin of safety" into procurement: (i) estimate a fundamental band from marginal production cost, stock-to-use and long-run real trend; (ii) increase forward cover (longer tenor, higher hedge ratio) when price is well below the band and reduce it when well above; (iii) never take cover below the volume-certain floor or above the volume-certain cap. The academic support is the value/reversal factor (modest, multi-year) plus storage theory. The evidence against is near-random-walk behavior at short horizons, the post-publication decay of value premia, and the selective-hedging literature showing no timing skill (Q1-D).
- Such a rule is speculation by Bartram/Stulz definitions and should be governed accordingly: a separate risk budget, ex-ante rules, and attribution against a passive hedge benchmark.
- Lynch-style "know what you buy" maps more naturally to *basis/spread* knowledge (logistics, quality and origin differentials), where agribusinesses actually earn margins (Q2), than to flat-price calls.

### Gaps
- No academic or industry case study of Buffett/Munger/Lynch-style heuristics applied by a corporate procurement function was found.
- The commodity-specific post-2013 performance of the AMP value factor was not isolated.
- No evidence was found on cost-curve-based "intrinsic value" trading rules for agricultural commodities.

## Q5. Behavioral biases in procurement timing (anchoring, regret, overconfidence) and decision-support remedies

### Takeaway
Controlled evidence exists for **anchoring** (newsvendor ordering; procurement auctions with real farmers) and for **overconfidence and self-attribution** in corporate hedging (gold miners). Training and feedback reduce these biases only modestly. Direct evidence on regret-driven hedge timing in commodity procurement was not found. The documented remedies are rule-based (pre-committed hedge ladders and programs), not discretionary.

### Cited Findings
- [PR-found.] Schweitzer, M.E. & Cachon, G.P. (2000). "Decision Bias in the Newsvendor Problem with a Known Demand Distribution: Experimental Evidence." *Management Science* 46(3) (title/venue from background; PDF verified). Subjects' orders are biased toward mean demand ("pull-to-center"), consistent with anchoring and insufficient adjustment, and feedback and training did not eliminate the errors — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Cachon_schweitzer_ms.pdf)
- [PR] Ferraro, P.J., Messer, K.D., Shukla, P. & Weigel, C. (2024). "Behavioral Biases among Producers: Experimental Evidence of Anchoring in Procurement Auctions." *Review of Economics and Statistics* 106(5): 1381-1392 (verified). Randomized starting bids in three auctions with commercial US agricultural producers shifted final bids (anchoring). There is weak evidence that contract experience modestly reduces the effect — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v106y2024i5p1381-1392.html); [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10420905)
- [PR] Adam, Fernando & Golubeva (2015, *JBF* 60): asymmetric response to speculative gains and losses (self-attribution / overconfidence) among hedgers — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbfina:v:60:y:2015:i:c:p:195-208)
- [PR-found.] Brown, Crabb & Haushalter (2006): firms reduce hedges after adverse price moves, which is "against theory" and consistent with loss-avoidance or regret about hedge losses — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jnlbus/v79y2006i6p2925-2950.html)
- [PRESS] Airline post-loss behavior (Southwest after 2015-16; IAG cutting hedges from about 90% to 40% after 2020) shows programs being scaled back after visible MTM losses — [Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2016/07/25/southwest-airlines-gets-burned-by-fuel-hedges-agai.aspx); [EuroFinance](https://www.eurofinance.com/news/covid-19-puts-airline-hedge-strategies-under-new-focus)
- [OP] Practitioner commentary that anchoring and framing (e.g., calling a category "commodity") shape procurement negotiations has no supporting data — [Logistics Viewpoints, 2016](https://logisticsviewpoints.com/2016/06/14/moving-beyond-madness-to-methods-in-supply-chain-decision-making/). Farm-press commentary uses airline hedge failures as lessons for ag hedgers — [Farm Progress](https://www.farmprogress.com/commentary/hedging-blunders-lessons-from-airline-fuel-hedge-programs); [Global Finance, "Never use a hedge to bet on markets"](https://gfmag.com/features/never-use-hedge-bet-markets)
- [CO] Company remedies as described: written policies that prohibit speculation, cap tenor and require pre-approved strategies (Starbucks), and volumetric plus VaR limits with daily measurement (ADM, Bunge) — see Q2 sources.

### Inferences
- Biases with direct evidence relevant to buyers: anchoring to reference prices or recent levels (Ferraro et al. 2024; Schweitzer-Cachon), overconfidence and self-attribution after lucky timing (Adam et al. 2015), and loss-driven de-hedging (Brown et al. 2006; airline cases).
- Decision-support remedies consistent with this evidence: (1) pre-committed layered hedge ladders (time-diversified cover) that remove discretionary timing; (2) any discretionary "view" overlay bounded by a band around the policy hedge ratio, with separate P&L attribution against a passive benchmark; (3) evaluation on the *all-in cost versus budget and its variance*, not on hedge MTM, to reduce regret-driven abandonment; (4) hedge volumes tied to firm or probabilistic demand, with options for uncertain volume (the airline 2020 lesson); (5) published model anchors (fundamental band, stock-to-use) shown with uncertainty intervals so buyers do not anchor on the last price.
- Training alone is unlikely to fix these biases, since Schweitzer-Cachon found feedback and training ineffective and Ferraro et al. found experience only weakly corrective. Structural pre-commitment is the evidence-consistent remedy.

### Gaps
- No experimental or field study of regret aversion specifically in commodity purchase timing was found.
- No study evaluating decision-support systems (dashboards, algorithmic hedge ladders) on procurement outcomes in food or edible-oil firms was found.
- Practitioner sources on anchoring in procurement are opinion pieces without data.
